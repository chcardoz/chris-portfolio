Model numbers are Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index scores from the cited snapshots. The benchmark and reasoning modes change over time, so I am treating them as directional rather than perfectly comparable.

I moved to San Francisco in November 2024. Within a month, I was inside an AI company at Founders Inc, watching the hardware team and everyone around them use Claude 3.5 Sonnet (16 on Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index), o1 (31), Cursor, coding agents, and model APIs heavily in real work. In December, I got a close look at hardware, from validating an idea to sourcing components to selling online. I learned how to solder, how to source components, how to make prototypes, and how people were serving model calls inside products. I also had an observability idea I did not pursue: using LLMs to inspect and improve the agent harnesses themselves, which in hindsight was more ahead of its time than I realized.

In February 2025, I left the hardware company for Ship It, a four-week Founders Inc sprint<Citation href="https://f.inc/shipit/directory" />, and the whole period felt like the start of vibe coding. The models were not suddenly crossing one clean line, but they were getting better month by month, and you could already see products forming around them: Lovable breaking out, Rork still up-and-coming, another text-to-app company around me using the AI SDK, and tools like Bolt, v0, Replit Agent, and Cursor making it feel normal to turn text into apps, websites, components, and prototypes<Citation href="https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/25/swedens-lovable-an-app-building-ai-platform-rakes-in-16m-after-spectacular-growth/" /><Citation href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibe_coding" />. During that month, o3-mini-high (25), Claude 3.7 Sonnet with reasoning (35), and GPT-4.5 Preview (20) had all entered the same Intelligence Index frame. I built [Fractal](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MopzFfYr97A&time_continue=0&source_ve_path=MjM4NTE) in that context, using Cursor to code it and Vercel's AI SDK across OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google providers to turn text into 3Blue1Brown-style educational videos<Citation href="https://vercel.com/blog/introducing-the-vercel-ai-sdk" /><Citation href="https://vercel.com/blog/ai-sdk-4-0" />. I launched it, got 11,000 views, and interviewed the people who used it.

For the next three or four months, I was at a company that was also near this text-to-mobile-apps shape. The product was different, but the loop felt familiar: model call, generated code, sandbox or preview, user sees the result, then someone fixes whatever broke. The founder was using the AI SDK. The SDKs were getting better. Cursor was getting better. And most of my own day was very simple: wake up, go in, find bugs, ask Cursor to help me fix them, and repeat. It was not romantic. It was just the actual work.

That stint also showed me other parts of making a startup that I had not really processed at the time. A lot of it was emotional durability: being able to sit with ambiguity, be wrong, get ignored, keep asking, keep shipping, and not let the whole thing collapse into your own self-image. I do not think I was reflecting on that while it was happening. I was mostly just trying to be useful. But looking back, that was part of the education too.

After that, from July to November 2025, I went into making videos. I spent months filming, editing, rewriting, cutting, and trying to get better. It was nice, and it was also tiring and repetitive in the way video is tiring and repetitive. I do think I have some skill there. Part of the pull was that making my own content still looks, to me, like one of the more plausible paths to financial freedom: make something entertaining, own the distribution, build an audience, and be less dependent on someone else's job opening. How reliable that path is in 2026 is another question.

Around the same time, I kept returning to the same technical loop: an SDK, a model, a sandbox for untrusted code, and a way to show the sandbox output back to the user. Vercel later made this pattern explicit with Vercel Sandbox in June 2025, describing isolated, ephemeral execution for untrusted AI-generated code<Citation href="https://vercel.com/changelog/run-untrusted-code-with-vercel-sandbox/" />. OpenAI's Codex research preview in May 2025 was another version of the same idea: a software agent running in a sandboxed cloud environment, taking tasks in parallel, and returning its work to the user<Citation href="https://openai.com/index/introducing-codex/" />. At the time, I did not have a crisp phrase for it. Now people would call a lot of this a background agent.

Claude Code was also around then, but early. Anthropic introduced it with Claude 3.7 Sonnet on February 24, 2025 as a limited research preview, a command line tool for delegating engineering tasks to Claude<Citation href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-3-7-sonnet" />. In May 2025, with Claude 4, they added background tasks through GitHub Actions and IDE integrations<Citation href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-4" />. I remember playing with Claude Code before it felt like the center of the world. The tool was pointing in the right direction before the model capabilities fully caught up.

The thing that did catch up was the length of useful work. METR's time-horizon framing is a good way to describe what I was feeling: the 50%-time horizon is the length of task, measured by how long a human expert would take, that an agent can complete with 50% reliability<Citation href="https://metr.org/time-horizons/" />. In normal language, the question is: how long can the model stay useful before it loses the plot? A few minutes becomes an hour. An hour becomes a few hours. Eventually you start to believe that a model can go away, work, get stuck, ask a question, continue, and come back later with something real.

Then late 2025 and early 2026 made that feel obvious to everyone. Claude Opus 4.5 came out on November 24, 2025, framed by Anthropic as a step forward for coding, agents, and computer use<Citation href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-4-5" />. Artificial Analysis put Claude Opus 4.5 in reasoning mode at 70 on its Intelligence Index at launch, behind Gemini 3 Pro and tied with GPT-5.1 high<Citation href="https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/claude-opus-4-5-benchmarks-and-analysis" />. Opus 4.6 (53) followed on February 5, 2026, with Anthropic saying it could sustain agentic tasks longer, operate more reliably in larger codebases, and use a 1M token context window in beta<Citation href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-4-6" /><Citation href="https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/claude-opus-4-6-adaptive" />. By April 2026, Anthropic's own system-card index listed Opus 4.7, and Artificial Analysis scored Opus 4.7 at 57<Citation href="https://www.anthropic.com/system-cards" /><Citation href="https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/opus-4-7-everything-you-need-to-know/" />. The exact arguments about Codex versus Claude Code are less interesting to me than the direction: the models are becoming more agent-like. They do things, then come back and bother you, instead of you constantly bothering them.

There was also the Ralph loop phase, which feels important in hindsight. The idea was almost stupidly simple: keep the coding agent running in a loop, give it deterministic feedback, and let it keep trying instead of treating every prompt as a one-shot interaction<Citation href="https://ralph-cli.dev/docs/core-concepts/ralph-loop/" />. That was one hint of what was coming. You could brute-force some persistence out of models that were not yet naturally long-horizon. The loop was doing some of the work that the model itself would later start to do.

OpenClaw was the more dramatic version of that shift. It reportedly started in November 2025 under the name Clawdbot, then became OpenClaw as people began treating it as an open-source autonomous agent platform that could connect models to the software and services around them<Citation href="https://www.techradar.com/pro/what-is-openclaw" />. That moment felt insane because it was no longer just a better chatbot or a better coding autocomplete. It was a shape. A local or cloud agent could sit there, run loops, call tools, touch your files, manage tasks, and keep going. And once the shape was visible, there were Claw variants everywhere. Claw was to agents what the chatbot had been to the previous generation of models: the interface pattern that made the capability legible.

One UX that makes this obvious is the text-message agent. Products like Poke put the agent inside iMessage, SMS, Telegram, and sometimes WhatsApp, so you can ask for something in a normal thread and get a result later<Citation href="https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/08/poke-makes-ai-agents-as-easy-as-sending-a-text/" />. That is a small interface change, but it changes the feel of the thing. It stops looking like a chatbot you sit in front of and starts looking like a person-shaped process running somewhere else.
